An archive, and a reading across four dimensions

This site is an archive of a Master of Arts thesis in New Media, Aalto University on Human Digital Twins in art. The thesis follows the concept of Digital Twin, from its pioneer engineering industry into the art field. It built a taxonomy of artwork and placed each of them on a spectrum across four dimensions. This thesis follows the human digital twin out of engineering and into art.

This thesis follows the human digital twin out of engineering and into art. Instead of sorting works into fixed types, it places each on a spectrum across four dimension: reflective, responsive, autonomous, and intra-active. Each scores from 0 to 5. The result is a profile, not a label: across 29 works ordered by year, recent twin-aware art registers on all four dimensions at once, where earlier digital doubles register strongly on only one or two.

01

The Digital Twin

A digital twin is a virtual model kept in continuous, real-time correspondence with a physical counterpart.

The concept has gained recognition over the past decade across manufacturing, agriculture, infrastructure and product-lifecycle management. Its definition keeps evolving, but one feature stays constant: the real-time synchronisation between a virtual model and the physical thing it mirrors. Its use beyond these established fields is still being mapped (Karabulut et al., 2024).

The synchronisation depends on dense sensing. Real-time systems draw on detailed digital information gathered through microprocessors and sensors that record sound, temperature, movement and heart rate (Swan, 2012; Poutanen, 2015), while people-centric sensing lets a system register the conditions a user moves through and contextualise the events around them (Gubbi et al., 2013; Poutanen, 2015).

Full citings on reference can be found on the thesis doc.

02

The Human Digital Twin

From machine to person

The human digital twin (HDT) is a virtual replica of a real-world human, as well as an emerging body of work within the broader digital-twin domain (Lin et al., 2024). Where a digital twin’s centre is on machines and non-living entities, an HDT represents a person not only through physical characteristics but through inner aspects: personality, behaviour, thought processes and skills (Wang et al., 2024).

Research is developing rapidly in healthcare and industry. What stays comparatively under-examined is the HDT in the creative sector, even as AI and automation spread through art and media production (Nader et al., 2022) - the gap this thesis addresses.

The body as interface

Human behavioural and biomechanical modelling is now technically operational: from individual behavioural style to player behaviour in VR to biomechanical movement - evidence that the human side of the twin can be built, not just imagined.

Boddington argues that our physical and virtual worlds are blending and reshaping identity as it becomes hyper-enhanced and multi-sensory: collaboration turns co-created and immersive, and the body itself has finally become the interface (Boddington, 2021).

03

Theoretical framework

The reading rests on three anchoring theorists and their contemporary extensions: Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of the lived body, Lacan's account of self-recognition (carried into avatar studies by Cleland, 2008), and Barad's relational ontology of intra-action (with Haraway's cyborg). Joined also by Hansen's reading of bodies in code, and by Hayles' theory of the distributed posthuman self, which the thesis retains throughout as a connecting thread across the autonomous and intra-active dimensions rather than as a ground for any single one. The reflective, responsive and intra-active dimensions are each grounded in one of the anchoring strands; the autonomous dimension draws instead on recent work on machine agency and the sense of agency.

07

Reading the taxonomy

The framework is a map of how each work engages the self - not a test of whether a work “is” a human digital twin.

Read together, the four dimensions and their application across twenty-nine works describe a field in motion. Ordered by year, the profiles widen: the early digital double tends to inhabit one or two dimensions: Telematic Dreaming is almost purely responsive, Ghostcatching almost purely reflective - while recent twin-aware work such as Holly+, Conversations with Bina48 or Voice In My Head registers across all four at once. Returning to the question that motivates the study - how a human digital twin's perceived agency bears on the subject's sense of self and bodily identity - the taxonomy does not so much answer it as make it legible and tractable. What the mapping suggests is that autonomy rarely unsettles the self on its own: in the works that press hardest on identity it travels with the intra-active dimension, where the apparatus dividing person from twin becomes part of the work's own content. The relation between perceived agency and the sense of self appears to be staged most sharply where the autonomous and intra-active dimensions rise together.
